Describe the concept of learning and learning approaches

1.1Trask+
Ln

What learning is

A machine learns when it gets better at a task from data instead of being given the rules. Every method in Trask follows one loop: predict, compare, learn.

Ch 2 · p 10–18Ch 3 · p 25Ch 4 · p 48–49

Learning, in one sentence

Machine learning is the part of computer science where machines learn tasks they were never explicitly programmed to do (Trask quotes Arthur Samuel's definition). Ch 2 · p 11

Trask's shorthand is monkey see, monkey do: the machine watches a pattern and copies it, either directly (supervised learning) or indirectly (unsupervised learning). Ch 2 · p 11

The learning loop

Predictpush the input through the model
→
Comparemeasure how far off it was (the error)
→
Learnturn each knob to shrink the error
↺ repeat for every example, many times

This three-step loop is the core of supervised parametric learning, and of every network in the book. Ch 2 · p 16 Ch 4 · p 48–49 Learning is trial and error: if the prediction was too high, the weight goes down; too low, it goes up. Ch 3 · p 25

Describe the difference between parametric and non-parametric learning

1.2Trask
Pm

Parametric vs nonparametric

Parametric models have a fixed number of parameters and learn by trial and error. Nonparametric models let the data decide how many parameters there are, often by counting.

Ch 2 · p 14–19

The definition

A parametric model has a fixed number of parameters. A nonparametric model's number of parameters is set by the data, so in principle it can keep growing. Ch 2 · p 14

Trask's analogy: fitting a square peg into the right hole. A baby jams it into every hole until it fits (trial and error: parametric). A teenager counts the four sides and looks for the four-sided hole (counting: nonparametric). Ch 2 · p 14

Parametric: a machine with knobs

Data goes in, is processed according to the angle of each knob, and a prediction comes out. Learning means turning the knobs. Everything the model knows is stored in the knob positions. Ch 2 · p 15

Example: predicting a team's chance of winning. If the win/loss record turns out to predict well, its knob is turned up; if the number of toes doesn't, that knob is turned down. Each knob is the prediction's sensitivity to one input. Ch 2 · p 15–16

Nonparametric: counting

A model watching a foreign streetlight counts how often each light is followed by cars going. Three lights give three counts, so three parameters. With five lights it would have five: the data decided. Ch 2 · p 18

The line is blurry: parametric models still size their inputs and outputs to the data (one input per pixel). And 'parameter' just means any number used to model the pattern: weights, counts, correlation coefficients. Deep learning is parametric. Ch 2 · p 18

Common examples outside the book. Parametric: linear regression, logistic regression, neural networks. Nonparametric: k-nearest neighbours (keeps every training point and compares new points with them), decision trees (grow more splits as data grows), kernel SVMs (the number of support vectors depends on the data).

Describe the difference between machine learning paradigms in relation to how they treat data and their application spaces

1.3Trask+
Pd

ML paradigms

Paradigms differ in what data they need: supervised needs inputs with correct outputs, unsupervised only inputs, semi-supervised a bit of both, and reinforcement learns from rewards.

Ch 2 · p 11–13, 17Ch 11 · p 201

Supervised: turn one dataset into another

Supervised learning learns to transform an input dataset (what you know) into an output dataset (what you want to know). It needs both, for every training example. Ch 2 · p 12

Trask's examples: image pixels → is there a cat; movies you liked → movies you may like; someone's words → happy or sad; weather sensors → chance of rain; raw audio → a transcript. Ch 2 · p 11

He calls it the bread and butter of applied AI: most real ML work ends in some kind of supervised classifier. Ch 2 · p 12

Unsupervised: group the data

Also transforms one dataset into another, but there's no right answer to copy. You tell it 'find patterns and tell me about them'. Clustering is the classic example: datapoints in, cluster numbers out. Trask suggests thinking of every kind of unsupervised learning as a form of clustering. Ch 2 · p 13

puppies · kittens→ group 1

An unsupervised parametric model has knobs per group, and outputs the probability (0 to 1) that a datapoint belongs to each group. Ch 2 · p 17

Other paradigms

  • Semi-supervised: a small labelled set plus a large unlabelled one (see SU5–7).
  • Reinforcement: no answers, only rewards from an environment after acting.
  • Self-supervised: the labels are made from the data itself, such as hiding a word and predicting it. This is how LLMs are pretrained. Trask's fill-in-the-blank network is an early example Ch 11 · p 201.

Design and analyse machine learning algorithms

1.4Trask+
Ds

Design and analysis

Design means choosing how to predict, how to measure error and how to learn. Analysis means checking that it learns, how fast, whether it diverges and whether it works on new data.

Ch 3 · p 22Ch 4 · p 49–76Ch 8 · p 148–152

Designing: three decisions

  1. Predict: what goes in, what comes out, and how inputs are combined (the network shape). Rule of thumb: give the network about as much information as a human would need for the same prediction. Ch 3 · p 22
  2. Compare: how to measure error. Mean squared error makes every error positive and makes big misses count much more than small ones. Ch 4 · p 50–51
  3. Learn: how each weight is updated to reduce the error. Trask calls this error attribution, 'the blame game'; gradient descent is the standard answer. Ch 4 · p 49

Analysing: does it actually work?

Trask analyses his first learning rule, hot and cold learning, and finds two problems: it's inefficient (several predictions per update), and with a fixed step size it can't land exactly on the goal, so it ends up bouncing around it. Ch 4 · p 55

Gradient descent fixes both by computing direction and amount from the error. Analysing it further shows it can diverge: with a large input every update overshoots and the error explodes. The fix is a learning rate, alpha. Ch 4 · p 72–76

Finally, test it on data it has never seen. A network can get all 1,000 training images right and still do much worse on new ones: it memorised instead of generalising. Ch 8 · p 148–150

More analysis tools: compare with a baseline (e.g. always predicting the most common class); choose metrics that suit the problem (accuracy, precision and recall for classes; MSE or MAE for numbers); use k-fold cross-validation for a more reliable score on small data; and consider cost: training time, memory, and how it scales with more data.
